It is very rare these days to find politicians whom I respect despite disagreeing with their ideologies. One member of this highly exclusive club is Andrew Hastie. I would not go so far as to say I'd like him to be prime minister, but I think he is the best person to lead the Liberal Party in its current state.

Also, I have to admit, he's rather handsome. He's got a rugged charm to him. It doesn't have any bearing on anything, but you can't deny that someone with a bit of charisma wouldn't hurt the Liberal primary vote.

### Hastie's Politics

Hastie is considered a member of the 'New Right' which is a subgroup within the Coalition's National Right faction. The New Right emerged over the last two years and it is a group that includes what former Liberal AG George Brandis has referred to as the more 'antic' elements of the Liberal Party (geddit? Because Alex Antic is one of its members). For example, Jacinta Nampijinpa Price, formerly from the Nationals, is a part of it, which should give a clear sense of what they stand for.

In case that doesn't, this is the 'Trumpian' wing of the Liberal Party. While they're equally as conservative as the National Right's 'Old Guard' when it comes to social issues (e.g. LGBTQ rights), they're less fiscally conservative. These are the types who are happy with government intervention into the market in order to bring back manufacturing. They shun the free trade and economic liberalism that have long guided Liberal Party policy, instead advocating for a more 'Australia first' approach. They're also the ones most in favour of large cuts to immigration.

Andrew Hastie has been described several times as a man of 'deep faith' and is a devoutly religious man who has said that his beliefs guide his politics. This of course ties into his social conservatism. It's also prompted journalists to ask if he is a creationist (as his father, a Presbyterian minister, is) who believes the Earth is only as old as the Bible says it is. Hastie dodged the question: "I’m not going to be drawn, like I said, on issues of theology. I’m interested in public policy. There’s no religious test in this country for public office."

Economically, he speaks fondly of the days when we used to "make stuff" (his words) and wants to rebuild the manufacturing industry. It's hard not to feel nostalgic for well-made Aussie products, but it is also incredibly difficult to achieve it without massive government subsidies or prohibitive tariffs: those industries failed because they were not profitable. As much as I enjoy snidely pointing out that the car industry was ended under Abbott, it had begun to collapse well before that and would have struggled to compete with foreign imports even if it had been propped up. It's not a bad idea to increase our sovereign manufacturing capabilities, particularly to avoid global supply chain shocks and given the rules-based world order is looking a little shaky, I just want it to be clear it is not an easy fix and we should have priorities in what we make that aren't just based on national pride (e.g. trying to make Holden cars again just because people like the idea of driving an Australian car).

Hastie is also strongly opposed to legislating a net-zero target. He believes it is more important for us to use our uranium and fossil fuel resources to achieve energy security than to reduce emissions. I think this is a dumb idea because we are a land blessed with the perfect conditions for renewable energy (lots of wide, empty land exposed to plenty of sun and wind), renewable energy is cheaper than trying to set up nuclear power (for which we have no experience making) and thanks to advancements in technology like battery storage, it is now quite reliable. To say nothing of our obligation to assist with combatting climate change.

As mentioned before, he's also proposing a reduction in immigration. Noteworthy here was an Instagram post he made where he claimed Australians were feeling like "strangers in our own home" and blamed the housing crisis on high levels of immigration (a questionable claim, and a very Hansonian one at that, but one that people readily accept).

You may be wondering, after all this, why I like him so much. Because honestly I do not agree with the bulk of his political beliefs. The reason is that Hastie is an intelligent and articulate man with strong morals.

Before his time in the Australian Defence Force, Hastie did a bachelor of arts, studying history, philosophy and politics. He is a well-read individual (some of his recent reads include _War and Peace_, _The Art of War_ and Saint Augustine's _Confessions_) and writes essays (literal essays). He's a man with brains, humility and sense, which are otherwise severely lacking in the opposition frontbench (James Paterson is really the only other source of intellectual heft, but he sits in the Senate).

As for his morals, Hastie was one of the witnesses in the Ben Roberts-Smith trial when the latter was accused of committing war crimes. The two had served together in Afghanistan and Hastie attested there was a "widespread" view that Roberts-Smith was a bully and spoke about certain rumours about his conduct. When asked if he disliked Roberts-Smith, Hastie denied it and said he instead he pitied him: "I pity this whole process. I don't want to be here. I was subpoenaed for this." He said it was "terrible" for Australia, that the SAS and had "touched a lot of lives, "But until we deal with it, we can't move forward."

Additionally, Hastie is the most vocal about refusing to work with One Nation. This may seem bizarre given he and Hanson have such similar policy platforms. The reason, I believe, is that he knows that to concede them legitimacy will spell the death of the Liberal Party. I have said it before, but the Coalition is most at risk by One Nation's rise, and their only hope of survival is if they can present themselves as the true alternative to Labor. If they accept One Nation as a part of their coalition, then they permit their supporters to vote for ON, and (again, as I've previously noted) they will now have a three-corner contest in rural or some suburban seats (or worse, if independents and Labor also compete). If they deprive One Nation of legitimacy, they can stem some of the bleeding, but Hastie obviously also believes that the politics of One Nation resonate with voters. It seems his plan would be to present the Liberals as the party that can actually achieve those policies and thereby restore voters' faith in the party and prove it has a clear sense of purpose and direction.

I do not think there are many other people currently in the Liberal Party who have could manage to do that. Few of them give the sense they carry true convictions like Hastie does (Tim Wilson definitely has strong convictions, but they're for free market libertarianism—the man sleeps under a portrait of Ron and Nancy Reagan and no that is not a joke—which is not going to win back their lost voters).

The other question you're probably asking is:
### Why is Hastie not leader of the Liberal Party?

During the first leadership vote after Dutton lost the 2025 election, Hastie stepped back because he knew his support would get split with Taylor's and he'd likely lose. In addition, his policies were likely seen to be a bit too close to Dutton's and there was a belief the party needed to moderate, which is why Ley won.

When Ley began to falter, again Hastie stepped aside to let Angus take the leadership. This time, the calculus was a little different: post-election, the libs were not in an unsalvageable position and Hastie would have imagined he could turn the ship around, but by the time of the second spill, things had gone from terrible to abysmal, with their vote share drifting to One Nation. In allowing Angus to lead, there are three possible outcomes which all benefit Hastie:
1. Angus fails miserably and gets booted out. In that case Hastie becomes leader.
2. Angus struggles, but survives until the election, which he loses. In that case, Hastie can challenge the leadership (assuming he manages to retain his seat).
3. Angus does well and wins the election. In that case, Hastie is positioned to act as the natural successor.
Hastie is young. He has plenty of time to become leader and he knows it. Taylor does not and he probably thinks it's 'his turn' to lead. If Hastie had contested the leadership, there was no way Taylor would have simply stepped aside. And if he had won, it'd be likely that the pro-Angus faction of the party would have undermined him.
Hastie is now placed to be the next leader of the party. Probably the only thing that could get in his way is if Josh Frydenberg decides to contest his seat at the next election and wins. There aren't many other good leadership options left: the Shadow Cabinet is pretty bare.

There is, however, one other thing that stands in his way: the aforementioned Ben Roberts-Smith trial.

There is a faction in the Coalition and its donors who are diehard supports of the alleged war criminal. These include such major figures as Tony Abbott (who I note is now president of the Liberal Party) and John Howard. Hastie could become leader in spite of this, but the Liberals have rejected quality leadership candidates for less.

This is also going to be a thorn in his side when it comes to winning over One Nation voters because they love Roberts-Smith. I saw a poll where 70% of ON voters said they viewed him favourably (he ranked higher in favourability than even Barnaby Joyce, and was second only to Pauline herself). There are those who see him as a victim, as someone who, despite being a decorated veteran, is being persecuted by the establishment. George Brandis recently wrote about this saying the Roberts-Smith cause has become "a metaphor for all the grievance, anger, contempt and alienation by which its supporters are driven". As I noted earlier, Hastie did not testify willingly, and obviously, being under oath, he was compelled to tell the truth, but he still suffers from One Nation's vitriol. Also note that the recent WA state by-election in Secret Harbor that One Nation won is within the boundaries of Hastie's own electorate of Canning. He himself could be at risk come the next election.

Even so, I think Angus Taylor is an idiot and I honestly can't remember the last time he said anything recently. He's abundantly ignorable. He should never have been leader, and hopefully will never become PM. Hastie, looks like he could be an actually competent leader and if they can't work that out they deserve their fate.

Watch them elect Dan Tehan instead.
