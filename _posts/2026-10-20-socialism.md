The leader of the Victorian Greens, Ellen Sandell, has announced a policy to trial 10 government-owned supermarkets. They would sell essentials for 30% less than Coles and Woolworths do. Similarly, the Greens-aligned thinktank, the Green Institute, led by ex-Greens MP Max Chandler-Mather, proposed the federal government could forcibly aquire 200 Coles/Woolworths stores which it would operate. This is openly an attempt to tap into the Zoran Mamdani populism vibe: the democratic-socialist mayor of New York won election by promising, among other things, government-run supermarkets as a way to reduce the cost of living.

This idea is obviously appealing when one believes that supermarket giants are price-gouging. If the government entered the market, they would not need to worry about making a profit, and so they could cut the margins incredibly fine (or even sell things at a loss) as they can gain revenue through other means (and I assume the Greens would say they could pay for it by taxing the 1%, putting in a tax on gas exports or scrapping AUKUS).

Now, this idea is perhaps too socialistic for Australia, at least for now, but it's not the stupidest idea. It also has some limitations which need to be considered.

###The Colesworth Duopoly

Australia has one of the most concentrated grocery markets in the world. Coles and Woolworths together account for about 64% of the market share of grocery sales. While foreign groups have tried to break into the Australian market, and we have independent grocers and markets, none of these have the reach and power that the big two have. The biggest issue for a newcomer is that Coles and Woolworths are already entrenched: they have their supply chains, their home brand products, their customer loyalty and their physical real estate. All of these things take considerable time and expenses to build up from scratch. Presumably, no overseas company thinks it is worth trying, especially because the big two can undercut them until they give up (whereupon they can increase their prices again).

Aldi has always struggled to break the grip despite its generally lower prices because it has a smaller range: customers will prefer the convenience of shopping somewhere they can buy all their groceries. Aldi also has a perception of being 'budget' or low-quality that the big two have, over time, begun to shed (just compare a newly-built Coles store to the old one on Sydney Road in Coburg). IGA, the other major player, is viewed more favourably in terms of its range and quality, especially in that it depends on the needs/desires of the local area, but they have a smaller footprint (as in, literally less stores). There are other competitors, like Costco, but that has barriers to entry in the form of a membership and very few locations. These limitations hinder their abilities to make further inroads into the market, and because so many people still shop at Coles and Woolworths, they don't have much of an effect on prices. Additionally, even if they do sell things for less than Coles and Woolworths, the fact that the big two can get away with higher prices means that the smaller supermarkets don't need to cut their prices too much to be cheaper, thus prices overall are not significantly reduced.

I note that this is all theory and the practice of what actually occurs with prices is more variable, particularly due to seasonality and availability etc. But the point stands that, even with a few competitors, Coles and Woolies do not feel pressured to lower prices.

Additionally, being so large, Coles/Woolies have a very unfair advantage, allowing them to engage in all sorts of [underhand tactics](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-02-20/woolworths-coles-supermarket-tactics-grocery-four-corners/103405054) that have increased prices for consumers and made it difficult for competititors to get a foothold. A government-owned supermarket, however, would have much more power. It could buy land fairly easily, negotiate fair prices to farmers, have instant name recognition, sell at a loss and accrue debt without significant concern.

So might it not be a great idea for the government to get involved?

###Issues

It is oft-noted that the profit margins in the supermarket business are very narrow, only about 2%. If this is indeed the case, it would suggest supermakret prices cannot be reduced by more than 2% before they start making a loss. It also means that, even if there were government stores selling for less, the effect it would have on the prices at other supermarkets is limited.

A more practical problem is that the government is not in the business of selling food. It lacks the experience to set up and run a supermarket, to manage the supply chains and so on. Obviously, they can hire people to do it, it would be a lot of work and investment.

Which brings me to the last of the big issues: increased government debt. I tend to take a Keynesian approach to debt, and Australia is doing pretty well compared to the US or Japan when it comes to our debt to GDP ratio, so it's not strictly a bad thing if we have to take on more debt. Technically this would be an increase in government spending, but it has the purpose of reducing inflation by indirectly controlling the market, so it should not be inflationary. In a way, it could, since people will have more money to spend on other purchases, raising demand elsewhere, so it may increase core inflation (total inflation minus food and energy). Additionally, since food costs are dependent on things like fuel and fertilisers (which have become expensive due to war-caused scarcity), this increase the demand such that the wholesale cost of food becomes higher, which would raise costs for private supermarkets and increase the debt the government will need to fund it (or force them to raise prices as well). It is difficult to say, and I'm not an economist so I'm not going to model this, but it's worth mentioning that the policy's desired effects could also come with unwanted outcomes.

It must be said, thiugh, thaat ensuring children from low-income families can be fed is a really good thing for society. More generally, if people can save money on essentials, it gives them more economic freedom (such as more money to buy a house or invest), which should improve their socio-economic mobility. The cost of essential purchases, being unavoidable, have the biggest impact on those with low incomes, similar to my discussion of the GST in my post about Howard's tax policies. It is, in a way, a fixed cost that must be subtracted from a person's income and which cannot be lowered below a certain point by the individual's actions lest they starve. The only way for it to actually decrease is by lowering costs or by government assistance (e.g. via programmes like 'food stamps' or free school breakfasts).

###The Deadening Hand of Socialism

Personally, I have often believed that, for the most part, essential services are best provided by the government at low costs (or free). The free market, on the other hand is better suited to handle goods and services which people do not depend on, that is 'wants' rather than 'needs', because those tend to involve innovations or trends to achieve competitiveness. In some cases, government-run services make clear sense, like healthcare or education, where the products or services are more or less the same wherever you get it from (the curriculum is fairly consistent across the state, for example). Unlike, say, fashion or technology, where every company has a unique niche. But food and housing, despite being essentials, are different in that people do have preferences. A house's value is location dependent, and (generally) each house is unique: if the government built and sold all homes at the same price, it would be difficult to manage the demand for preferred locations, or it would lead to a lack of diversity in home designs. Similarly, with food, people have preferences on brands, have different quality limits they'll accept, or just like less popular/common goods. This tends to be served well by the free market and if the government simply sold all goods it could lead to more of an 'averaging' of products. There is also seasonality and capacity at play: if the government made a certain crop really cheap and people started buying it in larger amounts, it would distort the farming/production side of things. Then there could be a situation where a poor yield leads to rationing as demand would still be high even as supply is low.

But that is very much taking the idea to the extremes, Mao Zedong-style. The suggestions from the Greens are not 'soviet-style central planning', they're a little more like public housing in my property example: the government does build homes which are affordable or free for those who need them, while those with the money to spend can freely choose from the houses available on the market. In this case, particularly the Victorian proposal, the government stores are not going to take over the whole market, instead they'll simply add a cheap option which people can choose should they wish. In addition, the presence of these government stores will mean the other supermarkets will be forced to lower their prices to remain competitive (or at least, bring down their margins on similar products, even if the rest remain unchanged).

Another point to be made is that the government could set up stores and then privatise them at a later date to a supermarket chain with a small (or no) share in the Australian market: by setting up the infrastructure, it would reduce the difficulty for competitors to grow their business and the government could recoup some of its expenses. I do not believe the Greens want this, and privatisation will probably lead to higher prices at some point, but more competition in and of itself should be sufficient to drive down food prices. I've been living in a small-ish German city lately which has at least five major supermarket chains operating in it, each with about the same number of stores, and the prices here are very cheap relative to Melbourne: I spent about €130 last month on food. Which is to say, I would welcome any initiative that aims to reduce the oligopoly in Australia.
