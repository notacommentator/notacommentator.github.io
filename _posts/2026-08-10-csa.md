About a month ago, independent MPs Allegra Spender and Zali Steggall announced the formation of a new political party: Community Strong Australia. This was perceived by many to be the 'Teal' party that people had been long been saying would/should arise to fill the gap in the political centre that resulted from the extinction of the Coalition's moderate faction. CSA is, however, far from the political earthquake that people who'd argued for a 'Teal' party had expected it to be. This is mostly because it currently only consists of the aforementioned two MPs. The 'Teals' did not coalesce into a single force, instead the majority of them chose to retain their independence.

All of this of course leaves one to wonder: is the birth of CSA a turning point in Australian politics? Will this party be electorally successful? Could it ever become a party of government?

But before we get to all of those questions, there's a much simpler one that I think needs answering:

## What are the Teals?

I have come to dislike the term 'Teal'. I think it's a bit of a lazy way to describe the group of independents that have risen over the past years. It conjures up an image of an independent who:
- Could have been a moderate Liberal in times gone by
- Is socially progressive
- Is economically conservative
- Supports action to combat climate change (and had campaigns funded by Climate 200)
- Wants tougher anti-corruption laws
- Is female
One issue is that many of the current independents in Parliament do not fit these classifications. The other is I think the situation is more nuanced even in the cases that ostensibly fit.

Personally, I'd group them into three categories:

### The Centrists
- Allegra Spender (Wentworth)
- Monique Ryan (Kooyong)
- Nicolette Boele (Bradfield)
- Dai Le (Fowler)
- Zali Steggall (Warringah)
- Sophie Scamps (Mackellar)
- Kate Chaney (Curtin)
- Kylea Tink (North Sydney, seat abolished)
- Zoe Daniel (Goldstein, lost seat)
- Kerryn Phelps (Wentworth, lost seat. Preceded the Teal movement, but there are similarities in their campaigns)

These are the ones I'd call Teal because they collectively do use teal for their branding (excluding former MP Kylea Tink, who wore pink, and I believe Phelps used purple) and they're the ones that the term was created to define. They often come from former Liberal strongholds in the cities. While they are the ones that best match the 'socially progressive, economically conservative' descriptor, this is still not particularly true.

Spender is pretty much the perfect example: her policy aims are pro-business ("Making Australia the best place to start and grow a business"), she talks of 'restoring fiscal discipline' and she is in favour of reducing income tax (she proposed a 'progressive GST' where the GST would be raised to 15%, but lower income earners would get money paid back). Even so, she does not seem to think government should be completely 'hands off' when it comes to the market: she's got policies like subsidies for rooftop solar, student debt reductions and government paying for childcare, among other things. Plus she supports a tax on fossil fuel exports. She describes her economic policy as coming from the 'sensible centre' and while everyone loves to claim they're from the sensible centre, I think that's fairly true in her case. That is, her policies are 'centrist' in the way centrism tends to be a bit neither here nor there. I don't think that's necessarily a bad thing, but it is a point worth making: these are not ideological hardliners and they are not singing from the Liberal Party economic songbook (perhaps the Malcolm Fraser edition, but that's been out of print since 1983).

Ryan and Chaney's economic policies cover pretty much the same points. Boele's webpage says she supports the work of Spender on tax policy, though her pro-small business page talks a lot more about cutting red tape (so does Chaney), and she also has a section about tackling the Colesworth supermarket duopoly. Scamps says the same sort of things as Boele, though she also mentions increasing the Medicare rebate. Steggall's policy leans more to climate- and energy-focused investment, along with reduced public transport costs.

Dai Le is possibly the most socially conservative: her website has a link to request a portrait of the king and she famously wore an Australian flag dress to the Midwinter Ball. She is a patriotic immigrant whose family built a life from nothing and who believes the individual needs to work hard rather than relying on welfare to get ahead. She's a bit like Spender in this regard (who is the child of Italian migrants who started a business). Even so, her voting record shows she still aligns with the above-mentioned MPs.

All in all, they're just moderate centrists who care a lot about climate change, cracking down on corruption and want to support small business. Some of their policies would excite Labor supports given the ALP's rightward drift.

Since we're talking about centrists, there's also Rebekha Sharkie, the former Nick Xenophon Team (later rebranded as Centre Alliance) MP. She's officially in the party still, but the party is now just her (she's just keeping the branding so she can retain access to the party's funds). Functionally, she is an independent who has managed to retain her seat of Mayo since 2018 and I'd say she is quite openly a centrist.

### The Rural Independents
- Cathy McGowan (Indi, retired. Preceded the Teal movement)
- Helen Haines (Indi, succeeded McGowan)
- Andrew Gee (Calare)
- Michelle Milthorpe (Farrer, failed to win seat but she is of the same mould)

There are only a couple of them, but they tend to have less of an emphasis on environmental issues and are instead focused on giving rural populations a real voice. They started the 'Voices of' movement which was set the groundwork for the Teal's election campaigns. I should probably call them the 'Oranges' since Haines, McGowan, Milthorpe and Gee all use orange for their logos.

Gee is actually an ex-Nationals MP who split from the party in protest of their stance during the Voice referendum and managed to retain his seat in 2025. While probably a little more conservative than Haines, they do have similar priorities.

It might also be worth mentioning Bob Katter, the member for Kennedy, here. It may not seem like he's got anything to do with the Teals, but he is another example of a rural seat electing an outspoken independent. Katter's policies are scattershot and contrarian (I'm tempted to call him 'agrarian socialist', but he is hard to pigeonhole), and although he is quite anti-renewable energy and socially conservative, in other respects he aligns fairly well with the other rural independents.

### The Progressives
- Andrew Wilkie (Clark)
- David Pocock (Canberra Senate seat)

These are also a small group. They're independents who are significantly on the left.

Wilkie has held his seat for a long time, and I would not call him a 'Teal', but I've mentioned Katter so I might as well discuss him too. Wilkie was formerly in the army, then was on the Office of National Assessments. He resigned from this position in protest of the Iraq War (being, as it was, built on fabricated intelligence). He then became a Greens candidate (running in Howard's seat), before leaving the party and running as an independent in the Tasmanian inner-city seat of Clark in 2010. He was one of the MPs supporting the Gillard minority government. In his policy, he is very much left-wing. I'd say he's a Green in pretty much everything but name (and colour: his campaign colour is also orange).

Pocock is a very tough fighter. He has probably more cut-through than all the other independents combined. While he shares a couple of policies with the centrist Teals, these tend to be the more economically progressive ones (such as a tax on gas). I've seen people accuse him of being a populist firebrand (i.e. he says popular, but unworkably simple solutions to problems to feed off grievances with the government), but I think that's an unfair assessment: he is just a man who believes strongly in fairness and has strong moral principles (he got kicked out of the Parliamentary Sports Club after he drew attention to the fact it was sponsored by the gambling lobby). Honestly, the more I read up on him, the less different he seems to be from the rest of the Teals except he doesn't try to make himself look like a wannabe Liberal.

As you can see, there is really not a whole lot differentiating the 'Teals' except their freedom to focus on their own priorities and messages. They gain their strength by positioning themselves as representatives of their communities, putting them first, which is perhaps why they do so well in formerly safe seats since those are the ones the major parties most overlook.

The benefits provided by this independence are why so few have chosen to join CSA. But there are many people (the sort who write letters to the newspaper) who have expressed their belief/hope that the Teals form a new, centrist party to provide a true replacement for the Liberal party (or at least, a replacement that isn't One Nation). So what exactly is CSA?

## Community Strong Australia
As mentioned above, the CSA consists of just two of the Teals: Spender and Steggall. Some MPs have said they will speak to their constituents before deciding to join, others have ruled it out completely at this stage, but this does make you wonder how effective the party will be if even their ideological allies do not want to join it. As I mentioned above, being a member of a party inherently introduces restrictions on an MP. CSA tries to balance this by having very loose policy guidelines. Their only four 'policy pillars' are:
- Sensible economic management
- Climate action
- Equality
- Integrity.
Steggall herself notes that "This is a centrist party, not a centre-right replacement", which I think nicely dovetails into my description of the Teal policies.

In all other matters except providing supply and confidence to a government (which is decided by a 75% vote of the party room), its members are free vote as they wish. There is also no leader: instead “parliamentary leaders will collectively lead”. It amounts to being more of a framework to support independents than a party in the standard sense. For those who don't get the reference in this post's title, Claytons is the 'drink you have when you're not having a drink'. So, CSA is thus the Claytons party: 'the party you form when you don't form a party'.

But there are certain advantages to being a party versus standing as an independent, some of those are related to logistics and funding (particularly since new donation laws have been introduced that leave individual campaigners worse off than parties), but there is one other, very significant advantage...

## The Senate

"I am the Senate." So said Sheev Palpatine in the 2005 film _Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith_. Shortly after, he noted he had "Power. Unlimited power!" The same could be said in Australian politics: an independent or minor party in the Senate holds much more power than that which they would wield should they sit in the House of Representatives.

The reason for this is fairly simple. In the past 45 years, there has only been one minority government (Gillard's in 2010), meaning any upper house independent has been more or less irrelevant for legislation (excluding the odd occasions where there are absentees or conscience votes). In that same period, however, a government has won a majority in the Senate only once (Howard in 2004). The reason for this is due to the more proportional nature of the Senate. Keating once called it the 'unrepresentative swill': because it is continuously populated by minor parties and independents who act as a thorn in the side of the government, forcing it to comply with the will of... well, _swill_. Honestly, the barrier of entry to the Senate is pretty low. It is not as easy as it used to be, thanks to the removal of group ticket voting (Turnbull got rid of it before the 2016 election) and the associated preference deals whereby parties would choose where voters' preferences would flow through a series of backroom deals. But it is still significantly easier: just compare the Greens' Senate result in 2025 (six seats) to their House of Reps results (one seat), when winning about 12% of the national vote for both houses.

Currently, Labor has 28 seats in the Senate (a majority requires 38). They pass bills by either watering them down and getting the Coalition to support them, or accepting the Greens' demands and using their numbers instead. On current polling, I do not see Labor winning significantly more seats in the Senate than it currently has. The Senate has half elections, so all the seats won at the last election are guaranteed to be there in the next government (unless there's a double dissolution election, which I do not believe the requirements for will occur). This limits the gains available for Labor, and given they only won 16 seats last time and will have to defend the 12 seats they got in 2022, I think you can do the maths and work out why I doubt they'll reach a majority.

If CSA manages to form a sizeable bloc in the Senate (anything more than four seats), they are likely to be a serious force. They would, presumably, be a little easier to work with than the Coalition or the Greens, and thus be a more appealing choice for Labor, ensuring they won't get sidelined. They'd also benefit from either a reduction or gain in Labor's seat total: if Labor gains five or more seats, then they could use CSA plus Pocock instead of the Greens; if they lose about three seats, then they'd need more than the current eleven Greens senators to reach a majority. Of course, all of these numbers are a bit hazy and are just to give you a sense of how even a small number of seats could potentially have a big effect. Especially because they would only need to achieve about 5-10% of the vote to get this outcome.

I ignored the effects this would have on a Liberal government, mostly because I do not see them forming government at the next election and I'm only looking that far ahead. But suppose they somehow won, be it in coalition with One Nation or not, then I'd imagine CSA would act pretty much like the moderate/progressive wing of the Liberal party used to and vote in line with the Coalition, except they'd be more vocal about things they disagree on.

You may be wondering why CSA needs to form a party to get a seat in the Senate. The reason is because of how Senate voting works. On election day, voters are presented with two methods of electing candidates: preferencing six parties above the line (ATL) or preferencing twelve candidates below the line (BTL). Most voters are intimidated by the ridiculously long list of Senate candidates and the act of having to choose twelve of them, so instead they just vote ATL. According to Antony Green, the number is above 95%. It seems easier. Personally, I think BTL is actually pretty easy given most parties run multiple candidates so you can get to twelve with even only three parties, while ATL you are forced to preference minor parties you've never heard of. But I digress.

To get a box ATL, however, one needs to be a member of a party. Otherwise, you get lumped in the 'ungrouped' (BTL only) section at the end of the ballot. Since you cannot do a mixture of ATL and BTL, it means an independent will typically miss out on a lot of voters' preferences. Being ATL makes it possible for an independent to be included in a major party's how-to-vote card for ATL, and so could give them the votes to reach the quota needed for a seat. In fact, Pocock himself has a one-man party, because he recognised how difficult it would be to gain a Senate seat without ATL votes.

Combined with the small but significant boosts from preferences ATL (and being on that side of the line at all), having a party also lets CSA advertise itself as a whole rather than having to campaign for specific candidates. If they can get some decent name recognition by the time the election comes around, they'd be in a good position. They'd likely appeal to disaffected moderate Liberal voters who'd preference them over the Coalition and centrist or swing voters who are fed up with the duopoly, but not stupid enough to vote One Nation (or who are concerned about the conflicting messaging on whether the Liberals would form government with One Nation). Since the Senate is split by state, that also affects the spread of their vote. I think they could win at least one seat each in Victoria and NSW, given most of the 'teal' electorates are in those states. South Australia is also a possible seat, and then there's WA and Queensland that I'd put in the maybe basket. ACT votes on both its senators every election. I doubt CSA would beat Pocock if they tried and the territory is too much of a Labor stronghold for the ALP to not win a seat there. NT is practically impossible and Tasmania is a bit weird, especially given its disproportionate seat allocation, but I wouldn't write it off. So, I'm giving them a minimum of three seats with room for maybe four more (which includes the possibility of gaining an extra seat in Vic or NSW). All in all, that'd be a pretty good result which they could definitely build on the following election.

## The House of Representatives

I think it is difficult for CSA and the other 'Teals' win any more seats than they have currently. By that I mean a net gain: they have a lot of marginal seats they could lose, although there are other marginal seats they could flip. Nicolette Boele won her seat (Bradfield) on the thinnest of margins, while Zoe Daniel lost hers (Goldstein) with only a slightly wider margin. Even so, the cratering of the Liberal vote gives them an opportunity.

The issue for CSA is that the types of voters the 'Teal' movement is associated with are not often the major bloc in an electorate. There may be plenty of voters out there who support them, but they'd be dispersed across the state (which is the other reason I think the Senate is where the exciting stuff will happen). Not to mention that the independents in some electorates may want nothing to do with the 'Teal' brand (Milthorpe in Farrer is the one that most springs to mind) and so CSA being a party will likely have no material benefit for them. Whether CSA can extend its reach depends largely on if people view it as credible or not. They have no incumbency advantage and not a lot in the way of legislative achievements. Their speeches during debates and perhaps some amendments they've proposed and gotten passed are really all they have, and while that is not nothing (since they've got a platform to call out the government and propose policies), they aren't quite seen as a political force in any practical sense.

They do have a chance of winning the support of Labor voters in Liberal-held seats as they may choose to tactically vote for them instead of the ALP in the belief it's the more likely way of ousting the Liberal. The voting data does seem to support that this occurs.

These are still early days: the party was announced a little over a month and a half ago and the AEC (as far as I'm aware) still has to accept its application. There is still too much uncertainty to know for sure whether we are seeing the birth of something that will upset the status quo (and maybe appeal to the desperate-for-anyone-else One Nation voters) or if it will be another flash in the pan like pretty much every other political party I've seen pop up over my lifetime. The difference here, though is that the Teals do present themselves as intelligent, effective and sensible. Not to mention they've shown impressive staying power.

Personally, my ideal parliament would be one of many minor parties and independents, each representing their community as faithfully as possible, since that's what a member of the House of _Representatives_ is meant to do. I think a party like CSA risks tearing itself apart if it becomes too large, since it gives its MPs perhaps a little too much freedom, but judging by the crop of Teal MPs that exist, I doubt they'd have too many disputes if they manage to select candidates carefully.

Centrism has a weird history in Australia. Everyone seems to want it, but centrist parties have never managed to take off. I think the issue has been in part due to poor party management and splitting the vote with one of the majors (resulting in the opposing side winning instead). But it's also perhaps because centrism is a bit of a blancmange: in trying to be all things to everyone, you do none of them well. Centrism is an ideology that makes it look like you have no strong convictions. In that way, it can be surprisingly populist, since it will go with whatever sounds good and 'reasonable' even if the reality of the situation does not allow it.

I feel the Teals avoid this by having certain firm planks, like climate action, in their policies, but things like 'supporting small business' or 'fiscal discipline' are political buzzwords. Their real strength does lie in their closeness with their communities.

I would say I doubt they'll be able to challenge the major parties, but the fact we're in an era where One Nation is outpolling the Coalition makes me think anything is possible. The next election is a long way away still, and a lot can happen before then. I will say I think we'd be more likely to see a Spender-Steggall duumvirate leading the nation before a Prime Minister Hanson. They are as likely as she is to end up being a deciding vote in the event of a hung Parliament.

Part of the issue with their centrism is that Albanese is currently working hard to make the ALP more of a centrist party and occupy that 'sensible centre' which the Liberals ceded. The Liberals have lost their moderates (the fact the media called Sussan Ley a moderate shows they're really scraping the bottom of the barrel) and are now in a weird state of trying to court the far right, so at least the Teals do not have to worry about them, but even so, as I've said, they're not really 'economically conservative' in the way Liberal voters tend to be. Is it enough to be 'modern Labor without the unions and with a different paint job' to attract Liberals? Or are they and Labor just fighting over the same set of voters? It is hard to say, but I do feel I need to point out that the electorates that were lost by the Liberals did still tend to have very large Liberal votes: they aren't as loved by socially progressive Liberals as people think.

With all that said and done, I welcome this party since I think the Teals are a group of sensible, well-meaning independents who would do a better job running the country than the Coalition. Hopefully they can help steer us away from the rise of radical, far-right populism. Because really, if the voters say their voting for 'anyone but' the majors and still vote One Nation (because Hanson 'can't be any worse' than the duopoly) when a party with about as much electoral and policy success as she has had, led by two people who aren't inarticulate xenophobes also exists, I think there's not much that can be done to save things.
