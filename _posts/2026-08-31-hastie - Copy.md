In the aftermath of the Nepalese floods and the release of the UN's recent report on climate change, _Limiting Overshoot_, I think it's a good a time as any to discuss one of the things I am most disappointed with about Australian politics: emission reductions.

The history of this is pretty short. John Howard refused to ratify the Kyoto Protocol (supposedly because emerging economies didn't pledge to do enough, but really I think he just didn't care to do anything about climate change). Kevin Rudd called climate change "the greatest moral challenge of our time" then ended up backtracking a bit when he got into power because of the political headwinds. He proposed a watered-down emissions trading scheme to the Liberals, which then-opposition leader Malcolm Turnbull was negotiating to possibly accept, but then Turnbull got ousted by Tony Abbott who refused it. The Greens wouldn't support it either since they wanted a proper carbon tax, but Labor decided not to work with them. And then Rudd got kicked out because he tried to impose a mining tax. When Gillard won with a minority supported by the Greens, she implemented a carbon tax. I'd like to pause here and acknowledge I used to believe the Greens were to blame for our lack of action on climate change because of their vote against the ETS, but I was like ten at the time so didn't learn the full details till much later. Obviously, they actually did get what they wanted in the end, and it was better than the ETS, but even if they'd accepted the ETS it wouldn't have mattered because it would have been doomed to be torn up anyway. Abbott campaign vigorously against the carbon tax, won the next election and scrapped it. Then he destroyed Turnbull's prime ministership over it, and then Scomo just bumbled around doing nothing much because he wasn't going to rock the boat. And now we're here where we are today.

I grow more and more frustrated with Albanese when it comes to efforts to meet our climate commitments. On the one hand, Labor is actually doing _something_ about increasing our renewable uptake and the fact is he is often hindered by opposition (e.g. protests about transmission lines or wind farms) that slows things down. But at the same time, he is dragging his feet and could go much further, particularly with regards to our mining and exporting of fossil fuels.

I appreciate the simple argument that if we don't sell coal to foreign countries, someone else will, and whether it's us or them won't change global emissions, and I also appreciate that there are Australian jobs involved in this and, perhaps more importantly, it provides a benefit for the economy (e.g. with taxes etc). I think some of this is overblown, but closing down the fossil fuel mining industry is a scare campaign waiting to happen which I think Labor considers would cause it to lose more supporters than it would gain. If the polls are trustworthy, Labor really isn't bleeding many votes to the Greens, so either its climate-conscious supporters are able to hold their nose and still vote for the ALP (out of dislike for the Greens or a preference for Labor), or there aren't many voters for whom it is a top-order priority. Plus, Greens votes will largely find their way back to Labor on preferences, so they can afford some defections.

Electoral calculus aside, we should be doing all that we can to mitigate climate change, and ideally we should even try to find ways to export our renewable energy, be it via undersea cables or by producing goods that require large amounts of energy and exporting those (e.g. smelting iron electrically instead of with coke, or by making ammonia for fertilisers).

Economist Ross Garnaut is a significant advocate for this sort of thing. He points out that Australia is naturally rich in sun and wind, so we have the opportunity to use these to our advantage. Australia has the opportunity to position itself as a key producer of materials in a decarbonised world. Energy-intensive products (like the aforementioned steel and ammonia) will need to come from somewhere, and we could be that place. With a well-managed transition, these new industries could counterbalance the jobs lost from the fossil fuel industry. But we need to be working towards this _now_. We've already wasted a decade or more treading water on this issue, and the longer we wait the harder it will be to make such a transition.

People on the right argue that Australia's emissions are a tiny fraction (about 1%) of global emissions and that what we do won't matter unless China and the US lower their emissions, on so we might as well not bother doing anything. The idea of Australia becoming a renewable energy 'superpower' (as Garnaut calls it) provides the strongest argument against this: if the world's industries shit to Australia because of our cheap, abundant renewable energy, then it will mean we'd be causing global emissions, not just our own, to reduce. According to Garnaut's own numbers, it could reduce global emissions by up to 10%. Albanese has made some progress on this front, like his Future Made in Australia policy. If you've forgotten about that, I don't blame you because it hasn't been a talking point for a while, but it is meant to incentivise certain investment in Australian energy and manufacturing, with an emphasis on renewables. It made for some lovely speeches, though I'm not sure if it's really as big a deal as it seemed at the outset. Still, a step in the right direction is always welcome.

Another annoying bugbear of mine is the current carbon trading scheme. This is what we've ended up with instead of a carbon tax. Essentially, polluting industries can buy carbon credits to offset the amount of carbon dioxide they've emitted. These credits are earned by other business who have done things to reduce emissions and since they get money from selling those credits, it's designed to encourage them to reduce emissions further. The issue is it leads to some dodgy accounting. For example, a farmer could earn carbon credits by not cutting down a certain amount of trees on their property, and then a power plant could buy these credits. But if the farmer was never going to cut those trees anyway, they're being rewarded for doing nothing. Consequently, no actual action has been taken to reduce emissions. It is electoral poison, especially now that all the parties of the right are opposed to net-zero, but the real solution here is a carbon tax.

If a company is made to account for the damage their emissions cause, then they will be pressured economically to shift their operations so as to produce less emissions. While new technology is expensive, by encouraging companies to invest and use these technologies, there will be a motivation to improve them and make them cheaper or more efficient. As things currently are, the barrier to making the change is too high since it is so much easier to just stick with things the way they are. Over time, the carbon tax will have less of a burden since companies will have changed their actions to minimise how much of it they pay, and as the new methods mature and become more economical, the costs caused by the transition will decrease.

We should also discuss the tax on gas exports that has been floated lately. This does not directly affect emissions, however if we gained some serious sovereign wealth from the mining industries (à la Norway and its oil industry), it would at least make them more justifiable. Polluting industries should be made to pay for the fact their products or processes cause social and environmental harm, to make up for the economic cost that is not accounted for when buying their goods. These funds could then be used by the government to invest in renewable energy or restoration of the environment. This tax is unlikely to eventuate in the near future because Albanese recently used our gas exports to leverage deals with Asian countries to continue supplying us with fuel when the Strait of Hormuz was shut. To add a tax to gas, and therby increase gas prices for Asia, would be a betrayal of trust. I think, because the tax is widely popular, it will happen eventually. It's just a matter of the Iran War being less of an issue (even if its consequences do not fully fade away).

But there is no appetite for a true 'carbon tax'. I'm honestly still angry with Abbott for killing that tax. As an eleven-year-old who cared about mitigating climate change, I was hopeful that the carbon tax was the beginning of a real change in Australia. Then we got nine years of nothing. It would take at least another ten years for a carbon tax to be politically acceptable, or however long it takes for enough voters to no longer remember or care about the 2013 election. But that would be too long and whatever right wing party is opposition by then will still probably rail against it, even if their harborside mansions or Ipswich fish and chip shops are submerged by rising sea levels.

I like to have hope, and I believe that Albo is a very tactical thinker. He often makes blunders, and he is incredibly stubborn, but I think he does personally care about climate change and he does want to see progress. I think he's willing to concede some issues in order to save himself from a united attack from the right and the mining lobby, while still making steady progress. He wants to make it look like he is not risking our energy security, even as the renewable share of our energy production increases. I just personally think he is dragging his feet and is too cautious.

That really is my biggest gripe with Albo. I get the idea of careful progressivism, and it feels very appropriate for Australia given how difficult it is for voters to accept radical change, and previous examples of fast-moving or bold governments ended abruptly (Whitlam and Keating), and Albo wants to make entrench Labor in government to give it the latitude to one day make these bold changes. But all of that aside, I think the PM does not fully appreciate that the mood of the nation (and in many comparable countries) is that the status quo is not working. This slow-and-steady approach just makes him look ineffective and flat-footed. Albanese is governing as if he were in 1999, in a peaceful, stable period of history where the economy is rosy and everyone is, as Howard said, 'comfortable and relaxed'. We are in a very strange and increasingly chaotic world, it is a much more difficult time to govern. Even if I believe Labor is doing just fine, considering the circumstances, their temperament is often out of sync with the public sentiment.

Since I brought up the fuel issue, a good example of this was the early Iran War panic: Albanese convened the National Cabinet, they settled on a plan, and then he made a special TV announcement that nothing was going to happen. Personally, I didn't care, because it seemed we _were_ in a situation where most of the danger was still hypothetical and a lot of the immediate problems were being caused by unfounded panic-buying. It would seem like a steady, calming response was needed, and a reminder that we could carry on as normal would be welcome. But no. People complained a bunch that he'd made an announcement that said nothing. In the end Albo was right: we never did need to implement fuel rations and his diplomacy in Asia was successful. But I don't think anyone is going to give him credit for that.

Part of this is the media's love of sensationalism, part of it is the right being rabid, part of it is the median voter's poor comprehension of politics.

I think bold action on climate change will probably do nothing to harm Albanese's image because it's already in the mud with the right and the left would love it. Albanese wants to be a leader for all Australians, to govern from the centre and bring the nation along with him as he makes progress and moves towards the future, but he's lost about a quarter of the population already (at least) and I don't think he's clearly not winning over the remaining Liberal voters, so he probably has not got as much to lose as he thinks.

At the very least, I am glad we have someone who'll openly acknowledge the issue. As Albanese said at the recent Pacific Islands Forum: “We all need to do more. Climate change is real, the science is real, the action that is required is real as well.” It's the absolute minimum, but there are still politicians in Parliament who would shy away from such a statement (and others who'd deny there even is a problem). This issue, especially in the context of Australian politics and the 'Climate Wars', just makes me so despondent. Like I said, I have hope Labor will continue to push forward until we get to a point where renewable energy is no longer an issue because we've just slowly become used to it being normal. But the time for that sort of incrementalism was a decade ago, when we had the time.

That's the story of Australian politics in a nutshell: everything happens a decade later than it should.
