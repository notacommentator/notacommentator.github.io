This is the first of a series of posts on all the things I blame on John Howard. If you speak to me about politics for long enough, you'll soon realise I really do not like John Howard and the rehabilitation of his image, where he is now viewed as one of the greatest PMs of all time, rankles me. Hence why I want to explain why exactly I think almost all of Australia's current political issues stem from the Howard years, either due to his policy decisions or his rhetoric.

The first thing I want to discuss is the Howard government's tax policies.

Why do we have taxes? It is a fundamental, but important question that I'd like to briefly discuss. There are two main uses for taxation. The first is revenue: the government needs to gain money for the purpose of providing services. The other is to manipulate behaviours: to encourage certain forms of economic activity by penalising unwanted actions. There is also a third reason, which is a little more subtle: the redistribution of wealth. That is to say, money is taken from the nation's citizens and given, through tax rebates, stimulus payments and subsidised services, back to certain groups of people. It's not always perceived as 'redistribution', and often the money is spread widely enough that it's not obviously being funnelled to any particular class or cohort, but it is redistribution nonetheless. I think it is always wise to consider, in the case of tax proposals, not just who the money is being taken from, but why and where it will go. And concurrently, it is important to consider the aforementioned concessions and payments to citizens as part of the broader tax ecosystem and not in isolation.

The Howard government pursued a tax agenda that would benefit those on the top at the expense of those on the bottom. It was very much 'trickle-down economics': the theory that more money in the hands of those owning and operating businesses will lead to increased productivity and growth (as the business will have more freedom to invest), and these gains will lift society as a whole. While this sounds lovely in theory, history has shown this tends not to happen in practice.

Howard was treasurer under Malcolm Fraser (my favourite Liberal PM) and was reportedly disappointed by his leader's decision to follow a more Keynesian approach to the economy (i.e. state debt can be used to encourage economic growth and so pursuing a balanced budget is not a major priority). Howard was then and is now a staunch fiscal conservative and a proponent of small government and after the Liberals returned to opposition, he, in his first, brief, stint as leader, shifted the party towards his views.

A proposed 15% goods and services tax was at the centre of the LNP's 1993 election campaign (as part of their 'Fightback!' policy platform) and it is said that they lost the moment Opposition Leader John Hewson could not explain in simple terms whether a birthday cake would or would not be subject to the tax [during a TV interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WndWM71-jSQ).

In light of this, John Howard promised voters during the 1996 election that there would "never, ever" be a GST. This was later revealed to be a "non-core" promise and Howard went to the 1998 election campaigning in favour of a 10% GST. He won that election and managed to implement the policy by winning over the support in the Senate of the Australian Democrats, after making a few concessions to them.

The politics and morality of breaking a promise aside, I am not the biggest fan of the GST. It is a broad-based consumption tax, a tax that everyone has to pay if they buy things. That sounds great, and it is one of the reasons I'm not seriously opposed to it: it is impossible to use tax loopholes and accounting tricks to get out of paying it (unless you buy nothing or only items exempt from it). The issue with it, however, is that everyone pays the same rate. The consequence is that people on lower incomes will have more of their earnings consumed by the tax. That is, it is a regressive tax. Since its amount depends on the price of the item you buy, and obviously people with more money can buy a larger amount of goods/services (and more expensive ones), they will tend to pay more GST, but the higher you go up the income ladder, the less of a portion of your wealth is affected.

I bring this up, not so much to hate on the GST, but because it is probably the most famous example of Howard's actions that brought about a more regressive (i.e. less progressive) tax system in Australia. But the GST is, on the whole, beneficial to the nation (even Keating once floated the idea of a consumption tax), particularly since it diversifies the government's revenue sources and so reduces its dependence on income tax. There are other, more egregious examples of Howard's economic decisions.

The next most famous (due to recent events) is the capital gains tax discount. You can see my post on the 2026 budget for more details on the CGT, but there I spoke mostly about it in regards to property investment. There is another use for the CGT and that is why there was such an uproar stirred up by a couple of business types after the budget.

The CGT is a tax on income derived from selling off an asset. This includes shares. If the income is high enough, then tax is applied. In doing so, people who earn money through _capital_ are still taxed like those whose earnings come from _labour_. Typically, wealthy individuals earn more of their money from capital (shares, property and other investments) rather than from their salary.

Essentially, in allowing people to claim a discount on the CGT if they own the asset for over a year, Howard made the tax system benefit those who are already own assets and who thus tend to already be wealthy. While there is some rationale for this (e.g. if a person is encouraged to invest in a business because they know they'll be taxed less for the income they earn when they sell the shares), it still leads to a distorted tax system, where the government is being disproportionately funded by income tax, which hits working people the hardest. The data shows that this is indeed the situation that we find ourselves in, where [income tax is by far the largest contributor to government revenue](https://treasury.gov.au/review/tax-white-paper/at-a-glance). More concerningly, a regressive taxation system that taxes wages more than capital/assets will likely result in a society with greater wealth disparity. Looking into the actual numbers, it is difficult to say whether there really has been a significant increase in inequality, but it does depend on the measurements used and the time scale of reference. If we compare pre-Howard to post-Howard, I believe the data is clear that it has increased, however I'm not an economist and I don't know if I can properly parse all the different ways of showing inequality.

One method by which Australia attempts to reduce the disparity in capital ownership, is through superannuation. It was a scheme implemented by Keating and Hawke and which the Liberals have done their best to weaken whenever they can. The employer contributes money to an investment account which the employee cannot access until they have reached retirement. In this way, it ensures all working Australians own capital (even if they cannot regularly access it). Thus, at least in their later years, Australians will benefit not just from their labour and income, but improvements in the stock market that might otherwise only be seen by the wealthy. One way Howard meddled with the system to the benefit of the rich was by exempting withdrawals from tax for those aged over 60. You see, super is not taxed as long as it is within the account (unlike if you had the money earning interest in a bank account). Howard's change made it so that someone on a high wage would not be taxed at all on a percentage of their income purely because they didn't touch it while it became more valuable. Keep in mind, of course, that super is otherwise taxed and that high-income earners can put more money into their super than those on lower wages (because they have more to spare to top it up). It was an overly generous system which benefitted the wealthy and which attracted an uproar when Jim Chalmers wound it back last year.

As I noted at the start, we should consider government handouts within the scope of the tax agenda to fully appreciate the flow of wealth in society. Like all prime ministers, Howard liked to give out economic 'sweeteners' in order to boost his popularity. The thing was, however, that Howard's economy was boosted by huge windfalls from the mining boom (which would have been even more beneficial if those industries were more appropriately taxed, but I digress) and by selling off public assets (which I will discuss at another time). Howard had the freedom to throw around money without worrying about a budget deficit. Consequently, his government created the political conditions which made it so difficult to reverse course in the following years.

The winding back of the CGT exemption (and negative gearing) this year is the first significant tax reform since the GST, which is to say that in the two decades since Howard was PM, pretty much nothing was done. Successive governments have been unwilling to legislate difficult tax changes because voters and vested interests had gotten used to the tax perks they have. When Labor proposed reforms to the mining tax, or a carbon tax, they were subjected to scare campaigns that destabilised and ultimately brought down the Rudd-Gillard-Rudd government. Similarly, neither Labor PM touched the superannuation tax exemptions (funnily enough, it was Scott Morrison who first wound it back a little).

While I do not think it is completely fair to blame Howard solely for the unfairness within Australian society today, his actions set it in motion and contributed to the subsequent governments' fears of taking any real action to combat it (although, I must say I don't think Tony Abbott was too afraid; I think he probably didn't care). He has left us with a system that rewards the rich at the expense of the poor and a political class too timid to take the action necessary to fix it. And yet, this is the least of his government's faults.
